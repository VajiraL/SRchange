# Phase 4: Scenario-Based Threshold Shift Analysis

## Overview
We applied the high-confidence thresholds ($R^2 \ge 0.8$) identified in Phase 2 to past (1901-1930) and future (2071-2099; SSP1-2.6 and SSP3-7.0) climate and land-use scenarios. The goal was to quantify how the spatial extent of "Saturated" adaptation regimes has changed historically and how it might evolve.

## Key Findings

### 1. Historical Trends (1901-1930 vs. 1991-2020)
- **Aridity Index:** The global area in the "Saturated" regime remained relatively stable (~46% to 45.6%), although regional shifts occurred as some areas became more arid while others became wetter.
- **Human Modification (gHM):** There was a significant historical expansion of the Saturated regime, increasing from **35.3% to 40.3%**. This reflects the global intensification of land use pushing ecosystems toward their adaptation limits.

### 2. Future Projections (2071-2099)
- **Aridity Index:** Under SSP3-7.0, the Saturated regime area *decreases* to **39.1%**. This counter-intuitive result is due to the definition of the threshold in some clusters: as regions cross a threshold into extreme aridity where $S_R$ can no longer increase, they are classified as "Saturated." If these regions transition to a different climatic state or if the threshold itself shifts (not accounted for in this static analysis), the interpretation changes.
- **Human Modification:** Future LU scenarios (SSP1-2.6 and SSP3-7.0) actually show a decrease in Saturated area relative to 1991-2020 in some model clusters, likely due to projected land abandonment or shifts in LU intensity patterns in those specific regions.

## Refinement Note
The counter-intuitive future results suggest that **static thresholds** applied to **static cluster maps** may not capture the full complexity of biome shifts. Future work should explore **dynamic clustering** (where grid cells can change cluster membership over time) to provide a more realistic assessment of future threshold proximity.

## Significance
This phase demonstrates that the "Adaptation Buffer" of the Earth's land surface is highly dynamic. While human modification has historically reduced this buffer, future climate and LU trajectories could lead to complex re-organizations of where ecosystems are most limited in their ability to adjust root zone storage.
